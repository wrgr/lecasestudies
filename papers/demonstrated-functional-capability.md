# Capability, Demonstrated

### Why the LENS concentration certifies what a graduate can *do*, not what they can *recall* — and why that is an engineering claim, not an educational one

*Discussion paper — LENS: Learning Engineering for Next-Generation Systems. Draft, August 2026.*

---

## Abstract

The LENS concentration makes a proficiency claim about its graduates: not that they have *studied* systems analysis, human-system collaboration, or decision-grade evidence, but that they can *perform* them, on a real problem, to a standard a reviewer can check. This paper argues that **demonstrated functional capability** — competence evidenced by a work sample scored against a task, rather than inferred from a test of knowledge — is the right basis for that claim, and that it follows directly from what LENS says capability *is*. It motivates the argument from the casebook: cases where a system passed every knowledge test, metric, or credential available to it and still failed the people it was built for, and cases where capability was instrumented and shown rather than asserted. It places the argument in the vocabulary of workforce analysis — the **KSAT** decomposition of a job into Knowledge, Skills, Abilities, and Tasks — and shows why a test that samples the Knowledge layer cannot license a claim about the Task layer. Finally it distinguishes the position from an educational one: LENS is not claiming to teach knowledge better than a test can measure it; it is claiming that capability lives at the *human-system interface*, that a performance gap there is as often a system gap as a person gap, and that only a demonstration in context can tell the two apart. The paper closes with how LENS operationalizes demonstration — pre/post objective pairs, capstone artifacts, a subobjective-keyed rubric, and an INCOSE/IBSTPI-style attainment profile — and with the limits of the claim, including the self-validation risk and the deliberately deferred question of credentialing.

---

## 1. The problem a test cannot see

A test is a *sign*. It stands in for a performance it does not itself observe, on the theory that someone who can answer the questions could also do the work. The theory is old, and its limits are older still. In 1968 Wernimont and Campbell drew the distinction the assessment literature has argued about ever since: between **signs** — traits and knowledge measured as predictors of performance — and **samples** — behavior elicited under conditions close enough to the job that the behavior *is* the performance. Work samples predict job performance better than sign-based tests because they ask a smaller question: not "does this person have the attributes we think the work requires?" but "can this person do a representative piece of the work?" Miller's 1990 pyramid put the same point in a clinician's terms — *knows*, *knows how*, *shows how*, *does* — and observed that most assessment stops two levels below the one that matters. A licensing exam certifies *knows how*. A patient needs *does*.

The gap between the two is not academic. It is the subject of the casebook this concentration is built around, and it recurs with a particular, teachable shape: a system clears every bar its builders set for it — an accuracy metric, a validation study, a certification, a vendor benchmark — and then fails in the field, because the bar measured a sign and the field demanded a sample.

Consider three, all from the casebook. **Watson for Oncology** (Case 3) was sold to hospitals worldwide on an advertised concordance with expert tumor boards as high as 96%, carried by the institutional credibility of IBM and its MD Anderson partnership; investigative reporting on leaked internal documents later established that it had been trained on a small set of synthetic cases rather than real patient outcomes, produced unsafe recommendations in documented instances, and was wound down as deployments were discontinued. The casebook's own reading is the sentence this paper turns on: *the institutional credentials did not substitute for the validation.* **The Epic Sepsis Model** (Case 5) was for four years the most widely deployed clinical AI in American medicine — embedded in hundreds of hospitals — before any independent evaluation was published; when Wong and colleagues finally ran one across 38,455 hospitalizations, the model's real discrimination was an AUROC of 0.63 against a vendor-reported 0.76–0.83, missing roughly two-thirds of sepsis cases at the operating threshold. It had cleared the only bar anyone applied — the vendor's own — and no other. **Houston EVAAS** (Case 60) is the cleanest instance: a proprietary value-added model attributed student test-score growth to individual teachers and drove termination decisions, until a federal court held in 2017 that a score no affected party could inspect, replicate, or contest could not support a consequential deprivation. The model produced a number every year; it never once produced a demonstration anyone could check.

The pattern is not that these systems were untested. It is that they were tested *at the wrong layer*. Each passed an assessment of a property — predictive accuracy on a retrospective cohort, alignment with a statistical model, conformance to a specification — that stood in for a capability nobody had yet demonstrated: that a clinician, or a teacher, or an operator, *working with the system*, could deliver the outcome the system existed to produce. The sign was green. The sample was never taken.

This is the failure a graduate of a high-consequence concentration has to be able to see coming. The claim of this paper is that a program cannot reliably produce people who can see it if the program certifies them the same way the failing systems were certified — by a test of what they know.

## 2. What LENS means by "capability"

The word does most of the work, so it has a definition of record. In the program documents, **capability is the interface between what a system requires of its human operators and the impact of that system** ([`2_LENS_Objectives_Course_Mapping.md`](../lens_program/2_LENS_Objectives_Course_Mapping.md), §1). It is not a property of a person. It is not a property of a machine. It is the *relation* between them, measured by whether the impact the system was fielded to deliver actually arrives.

Two consequences follow immediately, and both bear on assessment.

First, **capability is only observable in operation.** If capability is the operator-system interface under load, then a measurement that removes the load — a proctored exam, a multiple-choice item, a recall task — has measured something adjacent to capability, not capability itself. This is not a complaint about test validity; it is a statement about the referent. There is no "the capability" sitting inside the person waiting to be sampled by proxy, because half of it is on the other side of the interface.

Second, **a capability shortfall has more than one possible cause.** When the interface underperforms, LENS names four candidate sources — system design, human development, organizational structure, or their interaction — and treats telling them apart as a core competency in its own right: **gap attribution** (subobjective 4.2). "Differentiate capability gaps attributable to human development from those attributable to system design or organizational performance" is a required, assessed objective. A test of the *person* structurally cannot perform this attribution, because it holds the system constant and varies only the person, and so returns "person" for every gap it is capable of detecting. The instrument's design predetermines its finding.

These two consequences are why LENS's central pedagogical asset is a **casebook of demonstrated and failed capability** rather than a body of doctrine to be examined on. The evidence-capability-performance flywheel that LEN 1 introduces — faster evidence cycles accelerate capability development — is a claim about *doing and measuring*, iterated, not about knowing. The unit of the field's method is the iteration cycle (design, instrument, evaluate, refine), and the unit of its assessment is an artifact that shows one turn of that cycle completed.

## 3. The KSAT frame: tests certify the enablers, capability is the task

Workforce analysis has a standard vocabulary for decomposing a job, and it makes the layer confusion precise. A role is analyzed into **KSATs**: **K**nowledge (what a person must know), **S**kills (what a person can do with practice, usually psychomotor or procedural), **A**bilities (relatively enduring capacities the work draws on), and **T**asks (the discrete, observable units of work the role actually performs). The classic industrial-psychology triad was KSA (or KSAO, adding *Other characteristics*); modern occupational frameworks — the U.S. **O\*NET** content model, the **NICE** Workforce Framework for Cybersecurity, and the DoD Cyber Workforce Framework, which uses the KSAT acronym explicitly — carry the fourth element, **Tasks**, as first-class, because the task statement is the thing an employer can actually observe and an auditor can actually verify.

The structural relationship among the four is the whole point. **Knowledge, Skills, and Abilities are *enablers*; Tasks are *performances*.** A KSA is posited because it is believed to be necessary for some Task; it earns its place in the job model by the task it supports. This is why job-task analysis — the method underneath ISO/IEC 17024, the standard for bodies that certify persons — starts from the task inventory and derives the KSAs, not the other way around. The task is the criterion; the KSAs are hypotheses about what the task requires.

A conventional test inverts the derivation. It assesses the K layer directly — because K is the cheapest layer to write items for and the easiest to score reliably — and *infers* the Task. It says, in effect: this person holds the knowledge we believe the task requires, therefore we license the task. Every step of that inference is a place the field failures of §1 live. The knowledge may be necessary but not sufficient. The task may require an Ability the test never touched. The task may, in the operational setting, require *the system to hold up its half of the interface* — a condition entirely outside the person the test measured. The casebook even supplies the coefficient: the U.S. Navy's Digital Tutor produced apprentices who *outscored* fleet technicians with an average 9.1 years of experience on a knowledge test, at an effect size of 4.30 — and the case carries, unsmoothed, the finding that knowledge accounts for only about 40 percent of variance on the practical exercise. A spectacular result at the K layer is still a sign that leaves most of the sample unexplained.

LENS's assessment posture is the un-inverted one: **assess the Task, and let the demonstrated task performance stand as evidence that the enabling KSAs are present.** A work sample that requires the graduate to attribute a capability gap, instrument an intervention, and produce a decision-grade evidence artifact under irreducible uncertainty does not need to separately test whether they *know* what decision-grade evidence is. The knowledge is entailed by the performance, the way a builder's finished joinery entails that they can read a level. This is the same move the certification standards make when they insist on job-task analysis before a credential claim, and it is why the LECF crosswalk records that LENS is **not** attaching credential language to anything yet: the evidentiary bar for a certification — a validated job-task analysis under ISO/IEC 17024 logic — is a bar LENS names and declines to pretend it has cleared ([`4_LENS_LECF_Crosswalk.md`](../lens_program/4_LENS_LECF_Crosswalk.md)).

There is a second, quieter reason KSAT matters here. The DoD/NICE frameworks that popularized the fourth element did so because a *cross-functional* role — a leader, an integrator — is badly described by KSAs alone; what such a role contributes is legible only as tasks it performs and coordinates. LENS certifies exactly this kind of role: an **integrator** who can lead a learning-engineering team, not a specialist who holds one KSA cluster deeply. The attainment profile makes the integrator claim in the framework's own terms — practitioner on every *Individual* (floor) competency, awareness on the *Team* competencies a graduate briefs rather than performs ([`8_LENS_Attainment_Profile.md`](../lens_program/8_LENS_Attainment_Profile.md)). That is a task-and-role claim. A knowledge test has no way to state it.

## 4. Why this is an engineering position, not an educational one

It would be easy to hear all of the above as a familiar education argument — *authentic assessment beats multiple choice; performance tasks are more valid than recall.* That argument is true and is not the one LENS is making. The distinction matters enough to state plainly.

**An educational frame asks whether the learner acquired the knowledge.** Its object is the person; its success condition is a change in the person; its natural assessment is a measurement of the person, of which a test is one kind and a performance task a better kind. On this frame, "demonstrated capability" is simply a high-fidelity test of the individual — the top of Miller's pyramid — and the whole discussion is about instrument quality.

**An engineering frame asks whether the required impact is delivered at the interface.** Its object is the operator-system relation; its success condition is that the system's intended impact arrives; and its characteristic question, when impact does not arrive, is *gap attribution* — is this a person to develop, a system to redesign, an organization to restructure, or an interaction to re-engineer? On this frame a graduate can be fully "educated" — every KSA present and testable — and the capability can still be absent, because it was the system's half of the interface that failed. And conversely, the right intervention on a capability gap is frequently **not to teach the human at all**: it is to change the design so the human is not required to hold the failing KSA in the first place. This is the logic behind two of the casebook's six failure modes — *Designed Out* (the capability the operator needed was removed by a design choice) and *Human-System Interface* (the design demanded of the operator something no training could reliably supply). Neither is an education problem, and neither can be assessed, diagnosed, or fixed by a better test of a person.

Thomas Gilbert's *Human Competence* (1978) gave this its foundational statement a decade before Miller: worthy performance is an accomplishment relative to its cost, and the largest, cheapest gains in performance almost always come from the *environment* — information, instruments, incentives — rather than from the individual's repertory of knowledge and skill. Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model is, read one way, a formal argument that the education frame mislocates most performance problems inside the person. LENS's gap-attribution competency is Gilbert operationalized and made assessable: the graduate must *demonstrate*, on a real case, that they can locate a performance gap correctly before proposing to close it — and a demonstration is the only assessment that can score whether they located it in the right place.

This is the sharp end of the "different from education" claim. LENS stands *in* education — every student is in an M.Ed., the learning sciences are the foundation, learning engineering is the field it extends — but it works *on* the human-system interface in high-consequence domains, and it certifies a capability that is a property of that interface. A test measures a person. Capability is not, only, in the person. So a test cannot, alone, certify it.

## 5. What went right, and what went wrong: the casebook as the argument's evidence

The casebook is organized so the two halves of the argument sit side by side: *What Fails* and *What Works — and the Frontier*, in each of seven high-consequence domains. Read through the lens of this paper, the failures cluster as **sign-passed, sample-failed**, and the interventions cluster as **capability instrumented and shown**. A representative reading:

**Sign passed, sample failed.** Beyond Watson, Epic, and EVAAS (§1), two cases sharpen the diagnosis. **Pulse oximetry across skin tones** (Case 26) is a validation-architecture failure that ran for thirty years: a bedside device cleared on an aggregate accuracy number that concealed a group-specific failure mode, systematically under-detecting hypoxemia in Black patients because device validation was never demographically stratified. The sign — aggregate clearance accuracy — was not merely a weak proxy; it *hid* the capability gap it was supposed to certify. The aggregate number is not neutral; it is a construct choice, and only demographically stratified outcome evidence — the substance of the concentration's gap-attribution (4.2) and fairness-beyond-omission (4.6) objectives — samples the capability that matters. **Three Mile Island** (Case 166) looks at first like a training failure — operators cut the cooling the core needed — but the casebook attributes it elsewhere: a control room that reported *commands* rather than *states*, operators trained only for dramatic design-basis ruptures rather than slow ambiguous cascades, and a near-identical Davis-Besse near-miss that no channel ever carried to the fleet. Three of the six failure modes at once (Human-System Interface, a genuine Training Gap, and Knowledge & Institutional Memory) — and a better-tested individual operator would have prevented none of the structural three.

**Capability demonstrated.** The interventions show the un-inverted assessment working. **TREWS / Sepsis Watch** (Case 20) is the same delegation task as the Epic Sepsis Model and the opposite outcome — reduced mortality and organ failure in a prospective multi-site study — and the casebook locates the difference precisely: "the capability deliverable is the alert design, the clinician role, and the outcome-grounded evidence loop, not the model." The delegation was engineered *with its revocation* (subobjective 3.3) and instrumented so its effect could be shown rather than asserted; the honest hedge that RCTs remain pending is carried, not smoothed. **Crew Resource Management and CAST** (Case 117) is the field's clearest evidence that "capability is engineerable at the system level, not just the individual": Tenerife was not solvable by hiring better pilots, only by re-engineering the authority gradient inside the cockpit, and the paired cultural-plus-evidence intervention helped drive an 83% reduction in U.S. commercial-aviation fatality risk. A knowledge test of any single pilot would have measured none of it. **CIRCUIT** (Cases 68 and 78) closes the loop by making the paper's thesis its admissions policy: the workforce model *de-weights credentials* at intake in favor of demonstrable potential, then builds and evidences capability across six documented program cycles with high-resolution assessment; its MICrONS proofreading cohorts are a trained human correction layer deployed exactly where automation fails at petabyte scale. And the anchor case is the **Navy Digital Tutor** (Part IV, Defense & National Security): its graduates beat nine-year fleet veterans on the knowledge test *and* the program still refused to let the test stand for the capability, reporting the practical-exercise variance the knowledge score left unexplained. That refusal — to let an extraordinary sign substitute for the sample — is the discipline this paper argues a concentration must be built on.

The taxonomy the casebook scores each case against — six failure modes: **T**raining Gap, **D**esigned Out, **N**ormalization of Deviance, **H**uman-System Interface, **G**overnance & Trust, **K**nowledge & Institutional Memory — is itself an argument against the education frame's monopoly. Exactly one of the six, *Training Gap*, is the mode a better-educated (or better-tested) individual would have prevented. The other five are properties of designs, organizations, interfaces, and institutional memory — the environment side of Gilbert's model. A concentration that certified only against the *Training Gap* mode would be certifying against one-sixth of the failure surface its own casebook documents.

## 6. How LENS demonstrates capability

The position would be empty if the program could not act on it. It does, at four grains.

**Pre/post objective pairs.** The concentration states its effect as observable deltas — what an entering student can do versus what a graduate can do — written so the pre state is genuinely common at intake and the post state is scorable from artifacts ([`5_LENS_Summary_and_PrePost_Objectives.md`](../lens_program/5_LENS_Summary_and_PrePost_Objectives.md)). The pairs are deliberately behavioral: *attributes performance problems to training by default* → *diagnoses whether a capability gap is rooted in learning, system design, or organizational conditions before proposing an intervention.* Each pre state is measurable at entry by diagnostic scenario; each post state from a rubric-scored work product. The pair set doubles as the concentration's own decision-grade evidence of effect — the program instruments itself the way it teaches graduates to instrument a system.

**Capstone artifacts, assessed rather than asserted.** The LEN 10 capstone is an end-to-end learning-engineering project in a real organizational context, and it ships *signature deliverables* — an evidence dashboard, a reproducible report documenting at least one complete iteration cycle, and a governance/ethics plan. Every one of the five competency domains is, in the syllabus's own word, "assessed rather than asserted." The capstone is the work sample: a representative piece of the actual job, scored against the task, from which the enabling KSAs are inferred rather than separately examined.

**A subobjective-keyed rubric.** Because each competency decomposes into numbered, stable subobjective tags (4.2 gap attribution, 3.3 delegation with revocation, 3.4 collaboration measurement, and so on), rubric lines reference the tags directly, and the tags trace back to the cases that teach them. The rubric is the audit trail from a scored performance to the objective it evidences — the artifact an accreditor or an external site can check.

**An attainment profile.** Finally, LENS states the *level* a graduate reaches on each competency of the field framework — practitioner, awareness, or out-of-scope — on the framework's own Individual/Team warrant, following the INCOSE convention of proficiency levels per competency and the IBSTPI convention of individual competencies validated against practitioners. This is the claim in its most auditable form: not "our graduates studied these things" but "our graduates reach *this level* on *these* competencies, and here is where each is evidenced." It is a demonstrated-capability claim written for the audience most trained to distrust a knowledge test.

## 7. Limits, and what this does not claim

The position has real edges, and naming them is part of holding it honestly.

**It is not anti-test.** The Knowledge layer is real and worth assessing at its own grain; a task performance that entails the KSAs does not make the KSAs unimportant, and a program still owes its students formative knowledge checks along the way. The claim is narrower and load-bearing: a test of the K layer cannot, by itself, *license the Task-layer claim* the concentration exists to make. Signs inform; samples certify.

**Demonstration is expensive and harder to standardize.** Work-sample assessment trades reliability-per-dollar for validity; a capstone scored by a rubric is more susceptible to assessor variance than a machine-scored exam. LENS's answer is the subobjective-keyed rubric and the reproducible-report requirement, which exist precisely to make the scoring inspectable — but the honest statement is that demonstrated capability buys validity with effort, and the effort is the cost of the claim.

**The evidence risks being self-validating.** The competencies LENS most distinctively certifies — gap attribution, outcome coupling, capability at the human-system interface — are the field's *least* validated, flagged aspirational in the LECF crosswalk. If the only evidence that LENS graduates can do these things comes from LENS's own capstones scored by LENS's own rubric, the demonstration is circular. The stated mitigation is non-negotiable and external: recruit independent pilot sites so the practice evidence is not self-generated ([`4_LENS_LECF_Crosswalk.md`](../lens_program/4_LENS_LECF_Crosswalk.md), R3).

**Credentialing is deferred on purpose.** A demonstrated-capability *assessment* is not yet a *credential*. A credential requires a validated job-task analysis under ISO/IEC 17024 logic, and LENS explicitly declines to attach certification language until that bar is met. The attainment profile is offered as a proficiency *proposal* against the field's criteria, awaiting program-owner adoption and external validation — not as a certificate. Getting this order right, evidence before credential, is itself an instance of the discipline the concentration teaches.

## 8. In plain language

Students arrive knowing one part of the picture. They leave able to see the whole system, prove what works, tell a learning problem from a system problem, and lead the team that fixes it. A test can tell you they *studied* those things. Only a demonstration — a real problem, an instrumented intervention, an artifact a stranger can check — can tell you they can *do* them. LENS certifies the second, because the failures it exists to prevent were all, without exception, certified on the first.
