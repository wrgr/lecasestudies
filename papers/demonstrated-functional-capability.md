# Capability, Demonstrated

### Learning is what a person can *do*. A grade, a credit hour, and a passing score can each certify that learning happened while telling you nothing about whether it did. This is a paper about closing that gap — and a graduate concentration built to.

*Discussion paper — LENS: Learning Engineering for Next-Generation Systems. Draft, August 2026.*

---

## Abstract

A chemistry student scores 40% on the final; the curve turns 40 into a B; the transcript now certifies mastery. Nothing the student can *do* changed between the raw score and the letter. That gap — between the sign a credential records and the functional behavior it is taken to guarantee — is the subject of this paper. We argue for **demonstrated functional capability**: competence evidenced by a work sample scored against a real task, rather than inferred from a test, a grade, or a seat. The argument is not new to education — performance objectives, mastery learning, the clinical OSCE, the apprentice's masterpiece, and the pilot's checkride are all the field insisting that learning be shown as behavior — but the proxy keeps winning because it is cheaper. We motivate the stakes from a casebook of high-consequence systems that passed every bar their builders set and still failed the people they were built for; we give the argument a precise vocabulary through the **KSAT** decomposition of a job into Knowledge, Skills, Abilities, and Tasks; and we show why the LENS graduate concentration is built to certify the Task layer, not the Knowledge layer. The paper closes on the one turn LENS takes past even competency-based education: it locates capability at the **human-system interface**, so a performance gap can be attributed to the system rather than blamed on the learner — a diagnosis no test of a person can make. Throughout, the field's competency layer is treated as *emerging*, not settled: the Learning Engineering Body of Knowledge (LEBOK, lebok.ai) maps the knowledge; the competency and proficiency layer above it is still being built, and LENS offers its graduates' demonstrated work as evidence toward it.

## 1. The forty that became a B

A student earns 40% on a chemistry final. The class curve turns 40 into a B. The transcript now says the student has command of chemistry. Nothing the student can *do* changed between the raw score and the letter — a norm-referenced curve measured the student's *rank in a cohort*, not their command of a reaction. The grade is a **sign**, and a peculiar one, because it was engineered to float free of performance: it certifies that the student did better than enough classmates, in a room that may have collectively understood very little.

The chemistry curve is not an anomaly; it is the mechanism, in miniature, of how credentials decouple from behavior. **Partial credit** rewards a derivation that was started confidently and finished wrong — the vessel does not care that the first two steps were right. The **credit hour** certifies *time in a seat*: the Carnegie unit was adopted in 1906 as an administrative convenience for scheduling and faculty pensions, and the Carnegie Foundation that named it has spent years since saying it was never a measure of learning. A course that is **taught to its test** raises the score without raising the capability — the score and the skill come apart precisely because the score became the target, which is Campbell's Law and Goodhart's Law stated as a lesson plan. Each of these is a proxy that the real-world consequence never honors. The patient, the bridge, the cockpit, and the reaction vessel do not grade on a curve.

The distinction underneath all of them is old. In 1968 Wernimont and Campbell separated **signs** — traits, knowledge, and scores measured as *predictors* of performance — from **samples** — behavior elicited under conditions close enough to the job that the behavior *is* the performance. Miller's 1990 pyramid put it in a clinician's terms — *knows*, *knows how*, *shows how*, *does* — and observed that most assessment stops two rungs below the one that matters. Robert Mager had already written the corrective into instructional design in 1962: a real objective names *what the learner will be able to do*, under what conditions, to what criterion. Learning, on this view, is not a state inside the head to be inferred; it is a functional behavior to be shown.

Education knows this, and keeps re-discovering it. Bloom's mastery learning insisted the question is not *how you rank* but *whether you can yet perform*, and refused to move on until you could. Competency-based programs award progress for demonstrated competencies rather than hours. Medicine largely replaced the oral exam with the **OSCE**, a circuit of stations where a candidate *does* the clinical task under observation — an advance whose ceiling we return to in §5. The trades kept the guild's **masterpiece** — the journeyman certifies by making the thing. Aviation never used anything else: the **checkride** is a practical test in which you fly the maneuvers to a published standard, and no amount of ground-school knowledge substitutes for it. The through-line of every one of these is the conference's own premise — *learning demonstrated by functional behavior* — and the recurring failure of practice is that the demonstration is expensive and the proxy is cheap, so the proxy quietly wins.

When the proxy wins in a classroom, a transcript overstates a student. When it wins in a high-consequence system, people are hurt. That is where the casebook this paper draws on begins. **Watson for Oncology** was sold to hospitals worldwide on an advertised concordance with expert tumor boards as high as 96%, carried by the institutional credibility of IBM and its MD Anderson partnership; investigative reporting on leaked internal documents later established it had been trained on a small set of synthetic cases rather than real patient outcomes, produced unsafe recommendations, and was wound down as deployments ended. The casebook's reading is the sentence this paper turns on: *the institutional credentials did not substitute for the validation.* **The Epic Sepsis Model** was for four years the most widely deployed clinical AI in American medicine — in hundreds of hospitals — before any independent evaluation was published; when one finally ran across 38,455 hospitalizations, the model's real discrimination was an AUROC of 0.63 against the vendor-reported 0.76–0.83, missing roughly two-thirds of sepsis cases at the operating threshold. **Houston EVAAS** attributed student test-score growth to individual teachers and drove termination decisions on the number, until a federal court held in 2017 that a score no affected party could inspect, replicate, or contest could not support a consequential deprivation. Each system produced a sign on schedule. None produced a demonstration anyone could check. They are the chemistry curve with the stakes turned up.

## 2. What "capability" is, and why it cannot sit inside a test

If demonstrated behavior is the standard, the object being demonstrated needs a definition. In the LENS program documents, **capability is the interface between what a system requires of its human operators and the impact of that system** ([`2_LENS_Objectives_Course_Mapping.md`](../lens_program/2_LENS_Objectives_Course_Mapping.md), §1). It is not a property of a person. It is not a property of a machine. It is the *relation* between them, measured by whether the impact the system was fielded to deliver actually arrives. Two consequences follow, and both bear on assessment.

First, **capability is only observable in operation.** If capability is the operator-system interface under load, then a measurement that removes the load — a proctored exam, a multiple-choice item, a recall task — has measured something adjacent to capability, not capability itself. This is a statement about the referent, not a complaint about test quality. There is no "the capability" waiting inside the person to be sampled by proxy, because half of it is on the other side of the interface.

Second, **a capability shortfall has more than one possible cause.** When the interface underperforms, the source may be system design, human development, organizational structure, or their interaction — and telling them apart is itself a core competency in LENS, named **gap attribution** (subobjective 4.2). A test of the *person* structurally cannot perform this attribution: it holds the system constant and varies only the person, and so returns "person" for every gap it can detect. The instrument's design predetermines its finding. This is why the chemistry curve is not merely imprecise but *incapable* of the diagnosis that matters — a low class mean might be a cohort that did not study or a course that was not teachable, and the curve, by construction, cannot tell you which.

These two consequences are why the LENS concentration's central teaching asset is a **casebook of demonstrated and failed capability** rather than a body of doctrine to be examined on. The unit of the field's method is the iteration cycle — design, instrument, evaluate, refine — and the unit of its assessment is an artifact that shows one turn of that cycle actually completed.

## 3. The KSAT frame: tests certify the enablers; capability is the task

Workforce analysis has a standard vocabulary for decomposing a job, and it makes the layer confusion precise. A role is analyzed into **KSATs**: **K**nowledge (what a person must know), **S**kills (what a person can do with practice), **A**bilities (relatively enduring capacities the work draws on), and **T**asks (the discrete, observable units of work the role actually performs). The classic industrial-psychology triad was KSA (or KSAO, adding *Other characteristics*); modern occupational frameworks — the U.S. **O\*NET** content model, the **NICE** Workforce Framework for Cybersecurity, and the DoD Cyber Workforce Framework, which uses the KSAT acronym explicitly — carry the fourth element, **Tasks**, as first-class, because the task statement is the thing an employer can observe and an auditor can verify.

The structural relationship is the whole point. **Knowledge, Skills, and Abilities are *enablers*; Tasks are *performances*.** A KSA is posited because it is believed necessary for some Task; it earns its place in the job model by the task it supports. This is why job-task analysis — the method underneath ISO/IEC 17024, the standard for bodies that certify persons — starts from the task inventory and *derives* the KSAs, not the other way around. The task is the criterion; the KSAs are hypotheses about what the task requires.

A conventional test inverts the derivation. It assesses the K layer directly — because K is the cheapest layer to write items for and the easiest to score reliably — and *infers* the Task. It says, in effect: this person holds the knowledge we believe the task requires, therefore we license the task. Every step of that inference is where the failures of §1 live. The knowledge may be necessary but not sufficient. The task may require an Ability the test never touched. The task may, in the operational setting, require *the system to hold up its half of the interface* — a condition entirely outside the person the test measured. The casebook even supplies the coefficient: the U.S. Navy's Digital Tutor produced apprentices who *outscored* fleet technicians with an average 9.1 years of experience on a knowledge test, at an effect size of 4.30 — and the case carries, unsmoothed, the finding that knowledge accounts for only about 40 percent of variance on the practical exercise. A spectacular result at the K layer is still a sign that leaves most of the sample unexplained.

The un-inverted posture is the one LENS takes: **assess the Task, and let the demonstrated task performance stand as evidence that the enabling KSAs are present.** A work sample that requires a graduate to attribute a capability gap, instrument an intervention, and produce a decision-grade evidence artifact under irreducible uncertainty does not need to separately test whether they *know* what decision-grade evidence is. The knowledge is entailed by the performance, the way a builder's finished joinery entails that they can read a level. It is the same move the certification standards make when they require job-task analysis before a credential claim — and it is why LENS attaches no certification language to anything yet: the evidentiary bar for a personnel credential, a validated job-task analysis under ISO/IEC 17024 logic, is one it names and declines to pretend it has cleared.

There is a second, quieter reason KSAT matters here. The DoD/NICE frameworks that popularized the fourth element did so because a *cross-functional* role — a leader, an integrator — is badly described by KSAs alone; what such a role contributes is legible only as tasks it performs and coordinates. LENS certifies exactly this kind of role: an **integrator** who can lead a learning-engineering team, not a specialist who holds one KSA cluster deeply. Stating the *level* a graduate reaches on each such task — performs it independently, or briefs the specialist who holds it — is a task-and-role claim a knowledge test has no way to make.

## 4. Different from education as usually practiced — and one turn past competency-based education

It would be easy to hear all of this as a familiar education argument: *authentic assessment beats multiple choice; performance tasks are more valid than recall.* That argument is true, and it is not quite the one LENS is making. The concentration shares its floor with the best of the demonstrated-behavior tradition — Mager, mastery learning, the OSCE, competency-based education all assess what the learner can *do* — but it takes one further step that tradition does not, and the step is worth stating plainly to an audience that already believes the floor.

**Competency-based education demonstrates the individual's behavior.** Its object is the person; its success condition is that the person can now perform the task; its assessment, done well, is a work sample of that person. This is the top of Miller's pyramid, and it is a real advance over the grade and the credit hour.

**LENS demonstrates capability at the interface.** Its object is the operator-system relation; its success condition is that the system's intended impact arrives; and its characteristic question, when impact does not arrive, is *gap attribution* — is this a person to develop, a system to redesign, an organization to restructure, or an interaction to re-engineer? On this frame a learner can be fully competent — every KSA demonstrated — and the capability can still be absent, because it was the system's half of the interface that failed. And conversely, the right intervention on a capability gap is frequently **not to teach the human at all**: it is to change the design so the human is not required to hold the failing KSA in the first place. This is the logic behind two of the casebook's six failure modes — *Designed Out* (the capability the operator needed was removed by a design choice) and *Human-System Interface* (the design demanded of the operator something no training could reliably supply). Neither is an education problem, and neither can be assessed, diagnosed, or fixed by a better test of a person — however performance-based that test is.

Thomas Gilbert's *Human Competence* (1978) gave this its foundational statement a decade before Miller: worthy performance is an accomplishment relative to its cost, and the largest, cheapest gains almost always come from the *environment* — information, instruments, incentives — rather than from the individual's repertory of knowledge and skill. Gilbert's Behavior Engineering Model is, read one way, a formal argument that even a good performance assessment can mislocate a problem if it looks only inside the person. LENS's gap-attribution competency is Gilbert operationalized and made assessable: the graduate must *demonstrate*, on a real case, that they can locate a performance gap correctly before proposing to close it — and a demonstration is the only assessment that can score whether they located it in the right place.

So LENS stands *in* education — every student is in an M.Ed., the learning sciences are its foundation, learning engineering is the field it extends — and it holds the conference's premise harder than most practice does. What it adds is the engineer's move: capability is a property of an interface, not a possession of a person, so the demonstration has to be of the interface, and the diagnosis has to be free to send the fix somewhere other than the learner.

## 5. Exemplars: what the demonstration looks like, right and wrong

The everyday world is full of both halves of the distinction, and naming them is the fastest way to make the argument portable.

**Signs that certify little about behavior:** the curved chemistry grade; partial credit on a wrong derivation; the credit hour and the seat-time it counts; the driver's-license *written* test taken alone; the course drilled to its standardized exam until the score and the skill diverge (Campbell's Law made operational). **Demonstrations that certify behavior directly:** the road test that follows the written one; the clinical OSCE that replaced the viva; the journeyman's masterpiece; the airline **checkride** flown to a published standard; the CPR *renewal* that re-tests the compressions rather than trusting the card, because the skill decays whether or not the certificate does. The dividing line is exactly Wernimont and Campbell's: does the assessment ask about the person's attributes, or does it elicit a representative piece of the work?

The **OSCE** is worth dwelling on, because it shows both the promise and the ceiling of demonstrated behavior — and the ceiling is the part this audience should take away. Replacing the oral exam with a circuit of stations where the candidate *does* the clinical task was a real advance: the functional output — took the history, examined the patient, communicated the finding — is the thing that matters, and it is directly observed rather than inferred. But the OSCE also shows how a demonstration can quietly become its own kind of proxy. A station is short, standardized, and often checklist-scored, and those very properties reward **proximal** performance — the visible, atomized behavior at *this* station in *these* five minutes — while under-sampling the **distal** capability the behavior is supposed to stand for: the integrated clinical reasoning that transfers to an unstandardized patient, the knowledge that generalizes past the checklist, the downstream outcome the encounter existed to produce. Checklist scoring can even invert the signal, rewarding the exhaustive novice who runs every item over the expert who does less because they know which few things matter. So the lesson is not "demonstrate and you are done." It is that the *quality* of the demonstration — its fidelity to the real task, and whether it reaches the distal reasoning or stops at proximal task-completion — decides whether the sample is a genuine sample or a dressed-up sign. A demonstration assessed badly, or pitched at the wrong grain, is not automatically better than a good test; it is only better when it actually elicits the capability that transfers.

The casebook draws the same line at high stakes, and its two halves are organized to sit side by side — *What Fails* and *What Works — and the Frontier* — across seven high-consequence domains.

**Sign passed, sample failed.** Beyond Watson, Epic, and EVAAS (§1): **pulse oximetry across skin tones** is a validation-architecture failure that ran for thirty years — a bedside device cleared on an aggregate accuracy number that concealed a group-specific failure mode, systematically under-detecting hypoxemia in Black patients because device validation was never demographically stratified. The aggregate sign did not merely proxy weakly; it *hid* the capability gap it was supposed to certify, and only demographically stratified outcome evidence samples the capability that matters. **Three Mile Island** looks at first like a training failure, but the casebook attributes it elsewhere — a control room that reported *commands* rather than *states*, operators drilled only for dramatic design-basis ruptures rather than slow ambiguous cascades, and a near-identical Davis-Besse near-miss that no channel ever carried to the fleet. A better-tested individual operator would have prevented none of the structural causes.

**Capability demonstrated.** **TREWS / Sepsis Watch** is the same delegation task as the Epic Sepsis Model and the opposite outcome — reduced mortality and organ failure in a prospective multi-site study — and the casebook locates the difference precisely: "the capability deliverable is the alert design, the clinician role, and the outcome-grounded evidence loop, not the model." **Crew Resource Management and CAST** is the field's clearest evidence that capability is engineerable at the system level, not just the individual: Tenerife was not solvable by hiring better pilots, only by re-engineering the authority gradient inside the cockpit, and the paired cultural-plus-evidence intervention helped drive an 83% reduction in U.S. commercial-aviation fatality risk. **CIRCUIT** makes the thesis its admissions policy — the workforce model *de-weights credentials* at intake in favor of demonstrable potential, then builds and evidences capability across six documented program cycles. And the **Navy Digital Tutor** is the anchor: its graduates beat nine-year fleet veterans on the knowledge test *and* the program still refused to let the test stand for the capability, reporting the practical-exercise variance the score left unexplained.

The taxonomy the casebook scores each case against is itself an argument against the proxy's monopoly. Of its six failure modes — **T**raining Gap, **D**esigned Out, **N**ormalization of Deviance, **H**uman-System Interface, **G**overnance & Trust, **K**nowledge & Institutional Memory — exactly one, *Training Gap*, is what a better-taught (or better-tested) individual would have prevented. The other five are properties of designs, organizations, interfaces, and institutional memory: the environment side of Gilbert's model. An assessment that could only see the *Training Gap* mode would be blind to five-sixths of the failure surface its own casebook documents.

## 6. How LENS demonstrates capability

The position would be empty if the program could not act on it. It does, at four grains.

**Pre/post objective pairs.** The concentration states its effect as observable deltas — what an entering student can do versus what a graduate can do — written so the pre state is common at intake and the post state is scorable from artifacts ([`5_LENS_Summary_and_PrePost_Objectives.md`](../lens_program/5_LENS_Summary_and_PrePost_Objectives.md)). The pairs are deliberately behavioral, in Mager's exact sense: *attributes performance problems to training by default* → *diagnoses whether a capability gap is rooted in learning, system design, or organizational conditions before proposing an intervention.* The pair set doubles as the concentration's own decision-grade evidence of effect — the program instruments itself the way it teaches graduates to instrument a system.

**Capstone artifacts, assessed rather than asserted.** The LEN 10 capstone is an end-to-end learning-engineering project in a real organizational context, and it ships signature deliverables — an evidence dashboard, a reproducible report documenting at least one complete iteration cycle, and a governance/ethics plan. Every one of the five competency domains is, in the syllabus's own word, "assessed rather than asserted." The capstone is the work sample: a representative piece of the actual job, scored against the task, from which the enabling KSAs are inferred rather than separately examined. It is also LENS's answer to the OSCE ceiling of §5. Where a station is short, standardized, and proximal, the capstone is deliberately **distal and integrated** — one real problem in one real organization, carried through a full iteration cycle and linked to an operational outcome — so that what it samples is *transfer*, not a five-minute atomized behavior. The reproducible-report and rubric requirements are the other half of the answer: they hold the *quality* of the demonstration and its scoring open to inspection, so the sample cannot silently decay into a dressed-up sign.

**A subobjective-keyed rubric.** Because each competency decomposes into numbered, stable subobjective tags (4.2 gap attribution, 3.3 delegation with revocation, 3.4 collaboration measurement), rubric lines reference the tags directly, and the tags trace back to the cases that teach them. The rubric is the audit trail from a scored performance to the objective it evidences — the artifact an accreditor or an external site can check.

**A proficiency-level attainment profile.** Finally, LENS states the *level* a graduate reaches on each competency — performs it independently and produces evidence of it, or recognizes it and can brief the specialist who holds it — rather than reporting a pass. It follows two established precedents outside education's usual habits: the INCOSE systems-engineering convention of proficiency levels per competency, and the IBSTPI convention of individual competencies validated against practitioners. The field's own competency layer is still emerging — the Learning Engineering Body of Knowledge (LEBOK, lebok.ai) maps the knowledge, and the competency and proficiency framing above it is being worked out in the community rather than settled in a standard — so LENS states its profile as a proposal against that emerging consensus and offers its graduates' demonstrated work as evidence toward it.

## 7. Limits, and what this does not claim

**It is not anti-test.** The Knowledge layer is real and worth assessing at its own grain; a task performance that entails the KSAs does not make the KSAs unimportant, and a program still owes its students formative knowledge checks along the way. The claim is narrower and load-bearing: a test of the K layer cannot, by itself, *license the Task-layer claim* the concentration exists to make. Signs inform; samples certify.

**Demonstration is expensive and harder to standardize.** Work-sample assessment trades reliability-per-dollar for validity; a capstone scored by a rubric is more susceptible to assessor variance than a machine-scored exam. This is exactly why the cheaper proxy keeps winning in practice. LENS's answer is the subobjective-keyed rubric and the reproducible-report requirement, which exist to make the scoring inspectable — but the honest statement is that demonstrated capability buys validity with effort, and the effort is the cost of the claim.

**The evidence risks being self-validating.** The competencies LENS most distinctively certifies — gap attribution, outcome coupling, capability at the human-system interface — sit at the least-settled edge of the field's emerging consensus, where the demonstrated evidence base is thinnest. If the only evidence that LENS graduates can do these things comes from LENS's own capstones scored by LENS's own rubric, the demonstration is circular. The stated mitigation is non-negotiable and external: recruit independent pilot sites so the practice evidence is not self-generated, and offer it toward LEBOK's emerging competency work rather than banking it in-house.

**Credentialing is deferred on purpose.** A demonstrated-capability *assessment* is not yet a *credential*. A personnel credential requires a validated job-task analysis under ISO/IEC 17024 logic, and no adopted competency standard for learning engineering yet exists to anchor one. LENS declines to attach certification language until that bar is met. Getting the order right — evidence before credential — is itself an instance of the discipline the concentration teaches.

## 8. In plain language

A curve can turn a 40 into a B. A transcript can certify a degree. A benchmark can certify a model. None of them can tell you whether the learner, or the clinician, or the operator can actually do the thing when the stakes are real — only a demonstration can: a real problem, an instrumented intervention, an artifact a stranger can check. Education has always known this and keeps trading it away for a cheaper sign. LENS is a graduate concentration built not to make that trade — because the failures it exists to prevent were, without exception, certified on the sign and never on the sample.
